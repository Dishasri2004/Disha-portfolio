Human-Centered Design Case Study

MediFlow

Healthcare Appointment Experience Concept · Mobile UX Accessibility · IA · Research Plan

MediFlow is a concept for a healthcare appointment app. This case study shows how I would approach the problem as a human-centered designer: understanding context, making assumptions visible, structuring the experience, and planning validation. It is not a record of completed fieldwork or professional client work.

01 — Context

Why healthcare appointments?

Booking a consultation is often the first stressful touchpoint in care. People may be unwell, anxious, in a hurry, or helping a family member. The interface has to reduce uncertainty, not add to it. I chose this space because it rewards clarity, trust, and accessibility — the same priorities that matter in public-service and low-resource design.

02 — Problem

What makes booking hard today?

Looking at existing appointment flows, common friction points include unclear doctor options, hidden availability, too many steps before confirmation, and error messages that do not explain what to do next. The design problem is not just visual — it is about helping a stressed user make a confident decision quickly.

04 — Research Plan

How I would validate the design

This is a proposed research plan, not completed research. Before treating the concept as a solution, I would test assumptions through a mix of qualitative and quantitative methods.

Qualitative

Semi-structured interviews

Explore how people currently find and book care, what worries them, and where they give up.

Qualitative

Contextual inquiry

Observe booking in a real setting — at home, on a shared device, or while multitasking.

Quantitative

Short survey

Measure how often people abandon booking, which steps confuse them, and what devices they use.

Mixed

Competitive review

Compare existing appointment flows for structure, language, and error handling.

05 — Key Assumptions

What I am not sure about yet

Assumption: Users want to choose a doctor by specialization first.

Why it matters: It shapes the entire information architecture.

How I would test it: Card-sorting and tree testing with first-time users.

Assumption: A single primary action per screen reduces hesitation.

Why it matters: It drives the step count and visual hierarchy.

How I would test it: Moderated usability sessions comparing one-CTA vs multi-CTA layouts.

Assumption: People trust visible availability more than a “request callback” flow.

Why it matters: It determines whether slots are shown upfront or after selection.

How I would test it: A/B prototype test measuring confidence and completion.

Assumption: Hindi labels help comprehension for a segment of users.

Why it matters: It affects localization scope and content design.

How I would test it: Comprehension testing with bilingual and Hindi-primary users.

08 — Pain Points

Where confidence drops

  • Unclear next step: multiple equally prominent actions create hesitation.
  • Hidden availability: users cannot tell if a slot is real until late in the flow.
  • Unexplained errors: messages like “Invalid input” do not tell users what to fix.
  • No shareable summary: caregivers cannot easily pass confirmation details to the person receiving care.
09 — Problem Definition

The focused problem

Help a stressed or time-poor user confidently find a doctor, see real availability, and confirm an appointment — with minimal steps, plain language, and clear recovery from errors — across devices and digital comfort levels.

10 — User Flow

One primary path, with recovery

HomeOne clear CTA
→
SpecializationBrowse categories
→
DoctorDetails & trust cues

Each step has one primary action. If a slot is unavailable, the user is guided to the next nearest option rather than a dead end.

11 — Information Architecture

Why the structure reduces cognitive load

  • Home — one primary entry point, no competing CTAs.
  • Specialization — groups doctors by need, not by hospital structure.
  • Doctor detail — trust cues (name, specialization, availability) before action.
  • Time slot — availability visible before commitment.
  • Confirmation — shareable summary with all details in one place.

17 — Iteration

What would change after testing

If users hesitate at specialization

Test a search-first home instead of category-first, and measure time-to-doctor.

If errors cause abandonment

Move to inline validation and actionable messages, then re-test recovery rate.

If caregivers struggle to share

Add a one-tap shareable summary and confirm it is discoverable in a follow-up test.

If Hindi labels help comprehension

Expand localization and validate translated content with native speakers.

18 — Reflection

What this case study is — and is not

This is a concept exploration. It shows how I think through a human-centered problem: making assumptions visible, planning research, structuring information, designing for accessibility, and preparing to validate. It is not a claim of completed fieldwork, professional client work, or tested outcomes.

Next, I would run the proposed research and usability study, replace the proto-personas with research-backed ones, and iterate the design against measured results.
